# Across the United States Progression Redesign

## Purpose

This document proposes a simpler learner-facing progression model for **Across the United States**. It organizes the experience around what the learner is trying to understand rather than around the individual activity systems that provide instruction, practice, and assessment.

This is a product and design proposal only. It does not define final navigation, menu layout, unlocking rules, progress calculations, or implementation milestones.

## Design problem

Mappa Mundi has developed several useful U.S. learning systems, including Journey, Memory Trail, capital learning, physical-feature activities, Connections, Mental Map, Reconstruction, and Expedition challenges. Each system has a distinct educational or interaction role, but presenting all of them as peer destinations asks the learner to understand the application's implementation structure before understanding the learning path.

The proposed redesign keeps those activities and their distinct mechanics. It changes the learner-facing organizing idea from **choose an activity type** to **develop a kind of geographic understanding**.

The intended progression is:

> **Learn the pieces → Understand the systems → Use the map**

The learner should not experience Across the United States as a checklist of separate activities. Activities remain valuable, but they become tools supporting a coherent learning progression.

## Core progression model

### Stage 1 — Learn the Map

**Goal:** Build the basic mental map of the United States.

This stage establishes the political and spatial foundation needed for later physical geography and relationship reasoning. It includes:

- state locations;
- state names and identification;
- state capitals and state-capital associations;
- regional map reconstruction.

The learner should begin to see the United States as a connected arrangement of places rather than as 50 unrelated names and shapes.

#### Why regional reconstruction belongs here

Regional reconstruction is not only a late test of map knowledge. Used early and at an appropriate scale, it can be a learning tool that helps form the mental map.

Reconstruction:

- makes relative position visible;
- teaches how neighboring state shapes fit together;
- connects recognition of individual states to the structure of a region;
- transforms isolated memorization into a connected spatial model.

Regional scope keeps the task approachable while still requiring the learner to think about adjacency, orientation, and placement. Later reconstruction challenges may assess the same knowledge at greater scale or with less support.

Activities that may support this stage include:

- Journey-style state instruction;
- U.S. Memory Trail;
- capital learning activities;
- regional Map Reconstruction.

These activities should retain their distinct mechanics. The stage explains why the learner is using them and how each contributes to building the map.

### Stage 2 — Understand the Landscape

**Goal:** Understand the physical structure of the country.

Once the learner has a basic political map, this stage adds the physical framework around and through it. It includes:

- mountain ranges;
- major rivers;
- lakes and Great Lakes;
- coasts and major bordering waters;
- other major physical systems that help explain the country's structure.

The purpose is not merely to attach another list of names to the map. Physical features should help the learner understand why regions look and relate as they do. Rivers cross or border states, mountain systems organize broad parts of the landscape, lakes define important edges, and coasts connect the political map to larger water systems.

Existing physical-feature learning activities may support this stage. Their geographic representations should remain consistent with other contexts under the [Geographic Consistency Rule](geographic-visualization-principles.md).

### Stage 3 — Connect the Map

**Goal:** Understand relationships between places.

This stage moves from knowing individual objects toward reasoning about how they are connected. It includes:

- neighboring states and adjacency;
- state-to-state relationships;
- state-capital relationships;
- relationships between states and physical features;
- borders, coasts, and other geographic connections;
- routes and connected paths;
- direction and relative position.

Activities that may support this stage include:

- U.S. Connections;
- Mental Map relationship challenges;
- route and directional reasoning tasks.

The learner should increasingly be able to answer questions such as:

- What is beside this place?
- What physical system connects or separates these places?
- Which direction would I travel?
- How can I move from one state to another through connected states?
- How does a capital, river, lake, coast, or mountain system relate to the surrounding map?

This stage should develop a usable network of geographic relationships rather than a collection of disconnected factual associations.

### Stage 4 — Explore the United States

**Goal:** Combine and use the knowledge developed across the earlier stages.

This is the integrated Journey or Expedition layer. It should not feel like another menu containing the same activities under new headings. Instead, it should present a coherent sequence of challenges in which the learner demonstrates and applies multiple kinds of understanding.

Combined challenges may ask learners to:

- label states, capitals, or physical features;
- identify locations in response to spoken or written prompts;
- reconstruct regional or larger maps;
- answer connection and relationship questions;
- plan routes through neighboring states;
- use direction, adjacency, and physical geography to solve geographic problems.

The integrated experience may draw on existing activity mechanics without duplicating them or forcing them into one homogeneous gameplay system. Its value comes from composition: the learner must choose and apply relevant knowledge across a sustained geographic journey.

“Explore” in this proposal means active synthesis and application. It does not imply that Atlas browsing or open-ended exploration must be removed; those experiences may remain useful supporting paths.

## Relationship between learning and assessment

The progression should not assign every activity permanently to either “learning” or “testing.” Some activities can serve both purposes depending on when they appear, how much support is available, and what the learner is asked to do.

### Regional reconstruction

Early in Stage 1, regional reconstruction can function as a learning tool. Visible structure, manageable regions, correction, and repeated assembly help the learner form a spatial model.

Later, reconstruction can assess spatial understanding. Reduced support, broader scope, or an integrated challenge can show whether the learner can independently reproduce how states fit together.

The mechanic is the same, but its instructional role changes from supported construction to independent demonstration.

### Connections

Early relationship work can help teach and reinforce meaningful associations among states, capitals, physical features, and boundaries. Feedback can provide geographic context and help the learner understand the relationship rather than merely memorize an answer.

Later, Connections-style questions can contribute to demonstrations of geographic reasoning. When combined with route planning, direction, or map reconstruction, relationship knowledge becomes something the learner uses rather than only recalls.

### Evidence of understanding

The distinction between learning and assessment should depend on the attempt context and support provided, not solely on the activity name. Independent retrieval, assisted performance, guided exposure, and post-answer teaching may all be educationally useful while representing different evidence quality. The existing [Learning Support and Hint Philosophy](learning-support-and-hints.md) describes this distinction without prescribing a scoring formula.

This proposal does not change canonical evidence, scoring, or Progress Report policy. It identifies a learner-experience principle that future implementation work would need to respect.

## Progression without an activity checklist

The stages provide a comprehensible direction, but they do not necessarily require rigid completion of every supporting activity. Different learners may already know different parts of the map or may develop skills unevenly.

A stage could eventually communicate:

- the understanding it is designed to build;
- what the learner has already demonstrated;
- a useful current focus;
- recommended practice or learning experiences;
- opportunities to demonstrate readiness for more integrated work.

This preserves structure without reducing progression to time spent in each mode. A learner who demonstrates knowledge should not be required to repeat every introductory activity merely because it appears earlier in the proposed model.

The existing [Expedition Learning Model](expedition-learning-model.md) provides broader guidance for flexible progression, knowledge checks, and orchestration across distinct activities. This document applies those ideas specifically to the proposed Across the United States stage model.

## Future trail/menu implications

The future trail or menu could express the progression through a small number of learner-facing destinations rather than listing every activity system at the same level.

One possible conceptual structure is:

### Across the United States

**Learn**

- States & Capitals
- Physical Features
- Connections

**Explore**

- Regional or national journey
- Integrated challenges

This structure is illustrative, not a final interface specification. The final design might use a trail, map, cards, regions, milestones, or another navigation model. Submenus may be useful when they help the learner choose a topic or continue current work, but they should not recreate the original activity catalog one level lower.

Whatever interface is chosen should communicate:

- what kind of understanding the learner is developing;
- where they are within the larger U.S. journey;
- a clear recommended next step;
- available alternatives without overwhelming the primary path;
- how integrated challenges draw together earlier learning.

Direct access to individual activities may remain available through a secondary practice or exploration surface. The primary Across the United States experience should remain organized around learning goals.

## Open questions

- Should learners be able to skip or place out of stages through “Show What You Know” knowledge checks?
- How much freedom should learners have to move between stages before completing recommended foundations?
- Are the four stages sequential milestones, flexible areas of focus, or a mixture of both?
- Should all existing activities remain directly accessible, and if so, where should that secondary access live?
- How should progress be represented visually without implying that time spent equals understanding?
- What evidence should indicate readiness for the integrated Explore stage?
- How should regional choices appear in a future trail, particularly for state learning and reconstruction?
- Should physical-feature learning be organized nationally, regionally, or through a combination of both?
- How should integrated challenges vary support for learners who are still developing one component skill?
- What should happen when a learner demonstrates strong state knowledge but weak capitals, physical geography, or spatial reasoning?

## Design status

This document records a proposed learner-experience direction. It does not describe the current product as already following this structure and does not commit Mappa Mundi to a particular navigation hierarchy, lock model, activity sequence, or release plan.
