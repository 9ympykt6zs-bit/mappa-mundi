# U.S. Content Taxonomy

## Purpose

This document defines the types of contextual geography concepts that belong in the United States learning system.

Its purpose is to guide content creation, content audits, and coverage measurement so that Mappa Mundi adds material that helps learners build a useful mental model of the United States rather than simply accumulating trivia.

This taxonomy supports the requirements in `docs/US_DEFINITION_OF_DONE.md`.

---

## Core Content Principle

A contextual concept belongs in Mappa Mundi if it helps the learner better understand:

- where a place is;
- what surrounds it;
- what physical features shape it;
- why it matters;
- how it relates to other places;
- or how it participates in larger geographic, environmental, historical, cultural, or human systems.

Content should strengthen geographic understanding rather than reward memorization of disconnected facts.

---

## Content Categories

### 1. Physical Geography

Concepts about the natural physical features of a place and the landscape that defines or shapes it.

Examples include:

- rivers;
- lakes;
- mountain ranges;
- plains;
- deserts;
- plateaus;
- valleys;
- coastlines;
- islands;
- wetlands;
- major landforms;
- elevation patterns;
- watersheds and drainage features.

Examples of useful concepts:

- The Appalachian Mountains run through West Virginia.
- Florida is a peninsula bordered by the Atlantic Ocean and Gulf of Mexico.
- Colorado contains a major portion of the Rocky Mountains.
- Louisiana lies near the mouth of the Mississippi River.

Physical-geography concepts should emphasize features that materially influence how a place is understood.

---

### 2. Regional Relationships

Concepts that help learners understand where a state or feature sits relative to surrounding geography.

Examples include:

- neighboring states;
- relative direction;
- regional membership;
- border relationships;
- coastal relationships;
- proximity to major physical features;
- ordering states or features spatially;
- connections among adjacent geographic areas.

Examples of useful concepts:

- Indiana lies between Illinois and Ohio.
- Maine is the northeasternmost U.S. state.
- New Mexico lies west of Texas.
- Wisconsin borders both Lake Michigan and Lake Superior.
- Alaska is separated from the contiguous United States by Canada.

Regional relationships should help learners construct a connected spatial model rather than memorize locations independently.

---

### 3. Political Geography

Concepts involving political boundaries, administrative organization, and major political places.

Examples include:

- state capitals;
- major cities;
- state-region membership;
- national and international borders;
- Washington, D.C.;
- relevant territories or administrative divisions;
- relationships between political boundaries and physical geography.

Examples of useful concepts:

- Sacramento is the capital of California.
- Austin is the capital of Texas.
- Detroit is a major city near the Great Lakes.
- Texas shares an international border with Mexico.
- Maryland borders Washington, D.C.

Capital names should preferably be taught as relationships between a state and its capital rather than only as isolated city-location facts.

---

### 4. Human Geography

Concepts describing how people occupy, use, organize, and economically interact with geographic space.

Examples include:

- major population centers;
- settlement patterns;
- transportation corridors;
- agriculture;
- important industries;
- natural-resource use;
- ports;
- trade;
- migration patterns;
- urban development where geography helps explain it.

Examples of useful concepts:

- Much of Nevada's population is concentrated around Las Vegas and Reno despite the state's large area.
- The Mississippi River has historically served as an important transportation corridor.
- The Great Lakes helped make cities such as Chicago and Detroit major transportation and industrial centers.
- California's Central Valley is one of the country's major agricultural regions.

Human-geography concepts should remain tied to geography. Pure economic or demographic facts without meaningful geographic context are lower priority.

---

### 5. Historical-Geographic Significance

Concepts in which geography helps explain an important historical event, pattern, boundary, route, settlement, or development.

Examples include:

- migration and exploration routes;
- strategic rivers, passes, harbors, or coastlines;
- settlement patterns;
- territorial expansion;
- historical transportation routes;
- geographic causes of historically important events;
- boundaries shaped by physical geography.

Examples of useful concepts:

- The Mississippi River played a major role in trade, settlement, and westward expansion.
- Appalachian passes influenced routes of movement between the eastern seaboard and interior.
- New Orleans developed near the mouth of the Mississippi because of its strategic position for river and maritime trade.
- The Great Lakes formed an important transportation connection between the interior of North America and the Atlantic.

Historical content should explain geography or be explained by geography. Historical facts with no useful geographic relationship are outside the primary scope of this category.

---

### 6. Environmental and Geographic Systems

Concepts that show how places participate in larger natural systems.

Examples include:

- watersheds;
- drainage basins;
- climate regions;
- prevailing environmental patterns;
- coastal systems;
- Great Lakes systems;
- river systems;
- deserts and arid regions;
- mountain effects on climate and water;
- floodplains;
- ecosystems when strongly connected to geography.

Examples of useful concepts:

- Rivers east of the Continental Divide generally drain toward the Atlantic or Gulf of Mexico, while many western rivers drain toward the Pacific.
- The Rocky Mountains strongly affect water flow and climate across the western United States.
- The Great Lakes form the largest group of freshwater lakes by surface area on Earth and strongly influence the surrounding region.
- The Gulf Coast is vulnerable to hurricanes because it borders the warm waters of the Gulf of Mexico.

The emphasis should be on relationships and systems rather than isolated environmental facts.

---

## Content May Belong to More Than One Category

The categories are organizational tools, not mutually exclusive boxes.

A strong geographic concept may reasonably belong to several categories.

For example:

> The Mississippi River forms part of the border between several states and has historically been a major transportation corridor.

This concept includes:

- physical geography;
- regional relationships;
- human geography;
- and historical-geographic significance.

For coverage auditing, the same underlying concept should not be artificially counted as several independent concepts merely because it has multiple tags.

---

## State-Level Coverage Principle

Every state should have meaningful contextual coverage, but every state does **not** need equal coverage in every category.

Geographic importance should determine the mix.

For example:

- Colorado may naturally have substantial physical-geography and environmental-system content because of the Rocky Mountains and Continental Divide.
- Louisiana may naturally emphasize the Mississippi River, Gulf Coast, wetlands, settlement, and river trade.
- Delaware may have fewer nationally significant mountain or river concepts but stronger coastal, estuarine, regional-position, and political-geography relationships.
- Alaska and Hawaii require concepts appropriate to their noncontiguous and distinctive physical geography rather than artificial analogues to contiguous-state border questions.

The goal is meaningful geographic depth, not forced symmetry.

---

## Minimum Contextual Coverage

For the U.S. reference implementation, each state should have at least:

- one explicit state-to-capital relationship;
- one meaningful curated relational concept;
- and one meaningful non-capital contextual concept.

The non-capital concept should come from one or more of:

- physical geography;
- political geography beyond the capital relationship;
- human geography;
- historical-geographic significance;
- environmental/geographic systems.

This is a minimum floor, not a target maximum.

States with unusually important geographic relationships should contain greater depth.

---

## National and Regional Content

Not all important concepts belong to a single state.

The U.S. content system should also include concepts concerning:

- Census or other meaningful geographic regions;
- major river systems;
- mountain systems;
- Great Lakes;
- coastlines;
- plains;
- deserts;
- major watersheds;
- important transportation or settlement corridors;
- international borders;
- physical features spanning multiple states.

These concepts should be audited separately from state-level minimum coverage so that broad geographic systems are not lost in a state-by-state checklist.

---

## Content Quality Test

Before adding a contextual concept, ask:

> Does knowing this help the learner build a more useful mental model of the place, its surroundings, its significance, or its relationships?

A concept is strong when the answer is clearly yes.

Prefer concepts that:

- explain spatial relationships;
- connect multiple pieces of existing knowledge;
- explain why a place is important;
- help distinguish one place from another;
- connect political geography with physical geography;
- illuminate larger systems or patterns;
- are durable and broadly useful knowledge.

---

## Low-Priority or Excluded Trivia

Mappa Mundi should generally avoid facts whose primary value is novelty rather than geographic understanding.

Examples include:

- state birds;
- state flowers;
- slogans;
- mottos;
- arbitrary records;
- celebrity birthplaces;
- unusual laws;
- isolated dates;
- sports-team trivia;
- facts included primarily because they are surprising or amusing.

Such material may occasionally be appropriate if it directly reinforces a meaningful geographic concept, but it should not be used merely to satisfy a content-count requirement.

For example:

- “Georgia's state bird is the brown thrasher” would normally be excluded.
- “Georgia contains one of the country's largest Atlantic ports at Savannah, helping connect the Southeast to international trade” is geographically meaningful.

---

## Concepts vs. Question Instances

Coverage should be measured primarily in **unique learning concepts**, not raw question count.

One concept may support multiple forms of retrieval.

For example, the concept:

> The Colorado River flows through the Grand Canyon.

might eventually support:

- locating the Colorado River;
- identifying the river from the map;
- asking which river flows through the Grand Canyon;
- identifying which major feature lies along the river;
- using the relationship inside a puzzle or Mental Map challenge.

These are multiple question instances built on a shared underlying concept.

Rewording a fact several times should not make the content appear deeper than it is.

---

## Content Selection Priority

When deciding what content to add next, prioritize in this order:

1. Fill states or regions that fail the minimum contextual-coverage requirements.
2. Correct major regional imbalances identified by the content audit.
3. Integrate high-value concepts already present in atlas metadata but not yet taught or assessed.
4. Fill major gaps in national physical and regional geography.
5. Add additional depth to especially important geographic systems and places.
6. Add lower-priority enrichment only after the core coverage is strong.

Content development should therefore be driven by identified coverage gaps rather than by a predetermined total number of questions.

---

## Relationship to Adaptive Delivery

This taxonomy defines **what is worth teaching**.

It does not determine how frequently each concept should be shown to an individual learner.

The adaptive system may deliberately produce unequal question distributions when a learner demonstrates different strengths and weaknesses.

A balanced content inventory should provide appropriate learning opportunities across the country. Adaptive delivery should then decide which of those opportunities a particular learner needs.

---

## Future Evolution

This taxonomy is the initial standard for the U.S. reference implementation.

It should evolve when experience with real learners, geography pedagogy, content audits, or expansion to other regions reveals a meaningful limitation.

Changes should be made because they improve the educational model, not merely because new categories of trivia are available.
