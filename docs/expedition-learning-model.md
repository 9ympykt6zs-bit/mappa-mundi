# Expedition Learning Model

## Purpose

This document captures the long-term learner experience vision for large geographic learning experiences in Mappa Mundi. It applies to Expeditions such as **Across the United States**, **Across Central America**, **Across South America**, **Across Europe**, and future global Expeditions.

It describes product intent, not an implementation plan. It does not prescribe a particular interface, progression algorithm, activity sequence, or delivery milestone.

## 1. Expedition philosophy

An Expedition is a learner-facing structure that organizes a large geographic domain into a coherent journey. It gives learners a sense of place, direction, momentum, and purpose across multiple kinds of learning.

The trail is primarily a navigation and motivation layer. It helps a learner understand:

- what they have explored or demonstrated;
- what they are currently developing;
- what useful opportunities are available next;
- how individual activities contribute to broader geographic understanding.

An Expedition is not a fixed curriculum sequence and should not be understood as a checklist that every learner must complete in the same order. Learners enter with different histories. One learner may already recognize every state but know few capitals. Another may know political facts while struggling to construct a mental map. A third may have strong regional knowledge but limited experience reasoning about routes, borders, or physical geography.

The Expedition experience should acknowledge demonstrated knowledge and guide each learner toward useful next steps. Its goal is not **complete every activity**. Its goal is **develop geographic understanding**.

## 2. Expedition structure

An Expedition can present the domain through conceptual layers that move from foundational recognition toward richer geographic reasoning. These layers explain how different activities contribute to learning without requiring every learner to follow a single linear path.

### Recognize the landscape

- State or country locations
- State or country identification

This layer develops reliable recognition of the places that make up the domain. It gives the learner the basic visual and naming vocabulary needed for later relationships.

### Build the map

- Capitals
- Regions
- Neighbors

This layer connects individual places into a more organized political and regional model. The learner begins to understand where places belong and how they relate to nearby places.

### Understand connections

- Borders
- Rivers
- Mountains
- Coasts
- Other geographic relationships

This layer adds physical and relational structure. Places are no longer isolated shapes or names; they participate in systems, boundaries, landscapes, and regional patterns.

### Think geographically

- Routes
- Mental Map
- Reconstruction

This layer asks learners to use their knowledge rather than merely recognize it. They may need to reason about adjacency, direction, relative position, sequence, or the structure of a map as a whole.

These layers are conceptual, not necessarily mandatory stages. They can clarify increasing depth while still allowing learners to move between them according to prior knowledge, current needs, and interest.

## 3. Trail interface concept

The long-term Expedition interface may replace a traditional activity menu with a visual trail through the geographic domain. The trail should communicate a learner's relationship to the journey at a glance:

- **Completed areas** show knowledge or experiences already demonstrated.
- **Current focus** shows what the learner is actively developing.
- **Available activities** show meaningful choices that can be entered now.
- **Recommended next steps** provide guidance without implying that only one path is valid.
- **Optional branches** invite exploration, reinforcement, or deeper study without turning every activity into a requirement.

The trail organizes existing activities rather than replacing them. Journey-style activities, Memory Trail, Mental Map, Reconstruction, Connections, Atlas exploration, and future modes may retain distinct mechanics and purposes underneath the Expedition. The trail provides coherence across those systems; it is not itself a new gameplay engine.

This separation is important. A learner should experience one connected geographic journey without every activity becoming mechanically identical. The Expedition explains why an activity matters, where it fits, and what opportunities it opens next.

## 4. Flexible progression

Geography is not strictly sequential. Foundational knowledge helps, but geographic understanding develops unevenly across different dimensions.

A learner may:

- know all state locations but not capitals;
- know capitals but not physical geography;
- know political geography but have weak spatial reasoning;
- understand one region deeply while having little familiarity with another;
- recognize individual places but struggle to relate them to one another.

The same Expedition should therefore support multiple meaningful paths. Recommendations can respond to what the learner has demonstrated, what appears weak, and what kind of understanding would be most valuable to develop next.

Flexible progression does not mean that the journey lacks structure. The Expedition can still communicate dependencies, increasing challenge, and long-term direction. It simply avoids treating one universal activity order as a faithful representation of every learner's needs.

## 5. Locked content and knowledge checks

Locked nodes should represent meaningful guidance, not arbitrary restriction. A lock may communicate that an activity depends on concepts the learner has not yet demonstrated, or that another experience would provide useful preparation. It should not exist merely to force completion of every earlier node.

Where appropriate, a learner could bypass introductory material through a **Show What You Know** knowledge check. Such a check could sample multiple forms of knowledge, including:

- state or country location;
- state or country identification;
- capital relationships;
- route construction;
- map reconstruction.

The purpose of the knowledge check is placement, not punishment. It helps the Expedition recognize a knowledgeable learner and recommend an appropriate starting point. A learner who demonstrates advanced knowledge might open later opportunities while retaining access to earlier material for optional review.

Knowledge checks should support the same principle as the trail itself: progression reflects demonstrated understanding, not time spent moving through a fixed list.

## 6. Geographic reasoning

Recognizing a location and understanding geography are related but different achievements.

Consider the question:

> Where is Arizona?

This primarily tests recognition. The learner must connect a place name with its location or shape.

Now consider:

> Create a route from Arizona to North Carolina using connected states.

This can test several dimensions together:

- adjacency;
- regional understanding;
- directional reasoning;
- mental map construction.

The route question requires the learner to operate on relationships among places. It asks them to maintain a spatial model, choose connected transitions, and reason across regions. For that reason, successful route construction may provide stronger evidence of geographic understanding than isolated recognition alone.

This does not make recognition unimportant. Recognition supplies essential geographic vocabulary. The Expedition model should help learners progress from knowing individual places toward using those places in increasingly connected and transferable ways.

## 7. Guiding design principle

**A learner who already knows geography should not be forced through beginner material.**

The system should acknowledge demonstrated knowledge and help learners develop deeper geographic understanding.
